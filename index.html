Methodology

Everything on the page is generated from a small toy model of a single city laid out along one line, running from a dense core on the left to the far suburbs on the right. Here is exactly how it works. (Throughout, 1[…] is an indicator: 1 when the condition holds, 0 otherwise.)

1. Laying out the city

Each home and each destination is placed on the line at a distance x from the core. Positions are drawn from an exponential distribution, so places are common near the core and thin out with distance:

x = −L · ln(U), U ~ Uniform(0, 1)

The spread L sets the average distance from the core — a larger L pushes places further out, a smaller one packs them in. Homes and destinations each have their own spread, Lₕ and L_d.

2. How big each place is

Not every building is the same size. Each home i gets a population pᵢ, and each destination j an attractiveness wⱼ, both drawn from a heavy-tailed (log-normal) distribution so a few are far larger than the rest:

pᵢ = exp(σₕ · zᵢ) wⱼ = exp(σ · zⱼ), z ~ Normal(0, 1)

The inequality σ controls how lopsided the sizes are: at σ = 0 every place is identical; higher values let a handful of giant towers dominate.

3. Who commutes where

Every home sends its residents to exactly one destination, chosen at random but weighted by destination attractiveness — bigger destinations pull more commuters:

P(home i → destination j) = wⱼ / Σₖ wₖ

A destination's load is the combined population of every home assigned to it, and the city's total commuters T is the sum of all home populations:

loadⱼ = Σ pᵢ over homes i assigned to j T = Σᵢ pᵢ

Note that this choice depends only on destination size, not on where the home or destination sits — geography plays no part in who-goes-where.

4. The two density dials

Each slider folds spread and inequality into one "density" d from 0 (spread & even) to 1 (packed & towered), through a straight linear map:

Homes: Lₕ = 8 − 5.5·d, σₕ = 1.2·d Destinations: L_d = 7.5 − 6.5·d, σ = 1.3·d

Turning a dial up simultaneously pulls that population toward the core and concentrates it into fewer, taller buildings.

5. When a commute is "served"

A commute counts as served only when both its ends — home and destination — sit on the transit network. The two charts differ only in how that network grows.

Coverage by network size. The network connects buildings biggest-first. At size s, the s% of home buildings with the largest populations, and the s% of destination buildings with the largest loads, are on the network — a count of buildings ranked by size, not a share of the population. Because these are the biggest buildings, they hold well over s% of the actual commuters (at s = 50, the biggest half of homes holds roughly 70% of residents and the biggest half of destinations receives roughly 90% of trips). Coverage is then the population-weighted share of commutes with both ends connected:

C_net(s) = (1 / T) · Σᵢ pᵢ · 1[home i in top s%] · 1[its destination in top s%]

Coverage by core reach. Instead, a single line extends outward from downtown to a radius R, and everything inside is connected:

R = (s / 100) · X (X = full city radius) C_reach(s) = (1 / T) · Σᵢ pᵢ · 1[xᵢ ≤ R] · 1[x of its destination ≤ R]

In both, a served commute counts for as many people as live in that home, so coverage is measured in commuters, not buildings.

6. The skyline is stylised

The city view is a summary, not a literal scatter. The line is split into bins; within each bin the building heights are proportional to that bin's share of the city total, so the total height is conserved — spreading a population out makes all of its buildings shorter rather than rescaling the tallest one to fill the frame. Roof shapes and window textures are decorative.
